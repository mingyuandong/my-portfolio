Painting Series — Happening Changes

This series explores memory as an unstable psychological space, constructed through memory, imagination, emotion, and the inevitable gaps in recollection. Architectural structures and shifting geometries become metaphors for the mind’s attempt to reconstruct experiences that can no longer be fully recovered. Across the works, the past is neither fixed nor entirely accessible. Each act of remembering alters what is remembered, creating new versions of experience rather than restoring an original truth. The works therefore move between stillness and instability, physical space and psychological space, questioning our desire to preserve, control, and make tangible what exists only in memory. By treating memory itself as a medium, the series embraces distortion rather than photographic certainty. It asks whether reconstruction brings us closer to the truth of the past—or whether, in reconstructing what we remember, we inevitably invent both the past and ourselves.

Illustrations — The Child and Artificial Intelligence

Series The Only Real Person in the World explores the relationship between humans, robots, and perception. Drawing on solipsism and the thought experiment of the brain in a vat, the works question how we distinguish consciousness from appearance, and reality from what we imagine to be real. In an age of artificial intelligence, where images and experiences can be increasingly constructed, reality becomes not only something we encounter but something we interpret and create. Rather than seeking a definitive answer, the series embraces this uncertainty as a space for imagination, asking what it means to be human when the boundaries between the real, the artificial, and the imagined become increasingly fluid.

Series Feet in Water explores our relationship with nature through the imaginative world of childhood. As technology increasingly mediates how we see and experience the world, the works consider how our perception of nature can become distant from direct experience. Yet the series approaches this distance with optimism: through imagination, curiosity, and an innocent sense of wonder, nature can be encountered again in new ways. The child becomes a figure of possibility—someone who does not need to separate reality from imagination, but can move freely between the two. Feet in Water suggests that reconnecting with nature may begin not with recovering a lost past, but with learning to see the world with openness and wonder again.

Installation — Voico, The Say-See Diary
2025
PLA, acrylic, wood, and tangible media
4" x 7" x 11"

Voico is an interactive installation that quietly exposes how much we’ve surrendered our inner lives to algorithmic judgment. By translating spoken diary entries into shifting fonts, sizes, and pauses, the piece makes the machine’s interpretive power visible—and uncomfortably tangible. Instead of promising clarity or guidance, Voico foregrounds the strangeness of letting a device “decide” what we feel, revealing the gap between human nuance and computational certainty. The printed strips — emotive, inconsistent, sometimes wrong — form a physical archive of this tension, asking viewers to confront their reliance on smart systems to classify, soothe, or direct their emotional worlds. In the end, Voico becomes less a tool and more a critique: a sensory, poetic reminder that technology’s promise of understanding often masks its inability to truly know us.

Mixed Media Works — Behavioral Economics

Across my behavioral‑economics research, I use forms and constrained structures in paintings and installations to expose how human decision‑making is shaped by bias, assumptions, and societal influences. These pieces critique the idealized models of traditional economics and highlight the psychological forces that distort rational choice. By materializing theories into tactile, often unsettling installations, my practice encourages viewers to examine their own cognitive shortcuts, trade‑offs, and the invisible frameworks that guide their everyday decisions.

Ecocentric
2022
Metal, Yarn, Flashlight, Prefabricated Toy Train
30” x 30” x 70”

A welded metal structure casts a clock‑like shadow, revealing how economic choices always circle back to the self. The toy train and its enlarged silhouette evoke Plato’s cave, showing how our decisions shaped by upbringing, habit, and culture remain confined within personal “walls.” Ecocentric challenges viewers to step outside their own reference point and confront the egocentrism embedded in everyday judgment.

These works explore speculation through the lens of behavioral economics and optimism bias. While speculation often involves high-risk financial decisions made in anticipation of high returns, people tend to overestimate their likelihood of success. The works visualize and critique this tendency, examining how optimism can transform uncertainty and risk into perceived opportunity.

Installation — Collective Traces
2022
Paper, plaster, and hair
30" x 30"

Hair, growing from the head, becomes a metaphor for extensions of thought. Hair was collected from volunteers who received free haircuts, with each haircut separating layers of hair as if dividing layers of thought. Conversations during the process allowed participants’ thoughts to metaphorically enter the work. Once mixed together, individual identities become indistinguishable, forming a homogeneous collective. The work parallels this process with economics, where individuals are often simplified into collective groups based on shared choices and pre-established conventions. Although hair—and thought—appears light and flexible, both move through society according to recurring, sometimes imposed patterns. White tiles suggest waves and ambiguous directions, while rigid grids constrain the collective “thoughts.” At the same time, individual tendencies can become amplified within a collective.

Performance Installation — A Solitary Reverie
2021
Fabric, Ink on Paper, Video
40” x 80”

Sleep is a poetic process in which the brain relaxes and consciousness becomes detached from the surrounding world. Thoughts become more dissociative and arbitrary, yet can also feel more immediate, leading to an exploration of the self and an expanded state of mind. This project is an experiment in anti-painting. In a meadow near a forest, paint was poured onto a sheet of paper representing the freedom of exploration associated with sleep. The artist then slept on the paper. Rather than remaining the artist’s controlled medium, the paint assumed its own agency, moving and spreading around the body. Surrounded by rain and the impartial forces of nature, the sleeping body became part of an unpredictable process of making.

Dynamic Emotions
2021
Acrylic on canvas
8" x 80"

An exploration of the relationship between language and emotion through four vertically arranged paintings. Each panel represents a point along a spectrum, from the highest emotional intensity to the lowest, with the middle works serving as transitions. Their physical arrangement reinforces the contrast between extremes while emphasizing the continuity that connects them. Color, geometry, texture, and rhythm communicate emotional states. The highest point is energetic, passionate, and filled with visual tension, while the lowest is calm, quiet, and contemplative. Viewed together, the four works reveal emotion as a gradual spectrum rather than a set of opposites. The series questions the absolute nature of linguistic opposites. What defines high and low, beautiful and ugly, good and bad? By emphasizing the spaces between extremes, the work challenges fixed definitions shaped by social and cultural conventions.

Interactive Installation — Living House
2020
Cardboard
10” x 10” x 16"

This interactive work consists of an abstract structure and an arrow-shaped finder functioning as a flashlight. Participants can manipulate the finder from different angles to cast shadows from the structure. Only from one particular angle does the shadow reveal the form of an abstract house on the wall. The work explores the uncertainty of finding a place to live and the difficulty of defining and securing living space in underprivileged communities. The shifting relationship between structure, shadow, and perspective also evokes broader forms of economic and social inequality, as well as the ways that access to place can shape personal identity and belonging.
